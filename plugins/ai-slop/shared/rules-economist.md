# Writing rules: Economist layer

This optional layer loads with `--economist`. It distills the core writing
advice of Lane Greene's *Writing with Style: The Economist Guide* (2023),
especially the introduction and chapters 1 through 5. The rules and examples
below are written for this bundle. The book is not bundled or required at
runtime. Reasons for the choices are in `rules-rationale.md`.

## Scope and conventions

- **Apply the core advice to prose in scope** (`E.scope`). Apply this adaptation of Lane Greene's *Writing with Style: The Economist Guide* (2023) to documents, comments, commit messages, review reports, and assistant replies written or edited in Economist mode. Leave quotations, code, identifiers, commands, URLs, and other literal strings unchanged. In a review, apply the rules to the requested document, changed lines, or repository, as the selected workflow requires.
- **Preserve project and research conventions** (`E.precedence`). This layer adds editorial guidance to the general, scientific, and LaTeX layers. Their explicit conventions still apply, including American spelling, singular *data*, the Oxford comma, retained *that* in formal prose, terminology, citations, and statistical reporting. Project instructions and explicit user choices take precedence. `--economist` does not select British English or reproduce every Economist house convention. With `--ste`, also apply the stricter STE limits on sentence length, passive voice, and stylistic devices. Readability alone never justifies changing a claim, removing a qualification, or inventing a fact.

## Readers and words

- **Assume intelligence, explain unfamiliar knowledge** (`E.reader-knowledge`). Write for the intended audience without assuming that it shares the writer's specialist knowledge. Give an unfamiliar organization or concept its role when that role matters to the argument. Explain a necessary technical term in ordinary language on first use, then reuse the established term. For a concept mentioned only once, a plain explanation may be enough. Introduce an acronym only when readers need it again or already know it. Preserve precise terms and project glossary distinctions, and omit explanations that the intended readers do not need.
- **Choose familiar words without blurring meaning** (`E.precise-words`). Prefer the familiar word when it expresses the same meaning in context. Replace fashionable jargon, bureaucratic phrasing, and vague abstractions with the people, objects, or actions actually described. For example, "The team undertook the implementation of retries" can become "The team implemented retries." Keep an abstraction that is the subject of the argument and a technical term that carries a necessary distinction. Do not turn a measured association into an action or a broad category into a specific accusation for a livelier sentence.

## Sentences and argument

- **Make actors and actions clear** (`E.actors-and-actions`). When who did what matters, name the actor supported by the text and put the action in the verb. For example, "The maintainers carried out the rejection of the patch" can become "The maintainers rejected the patch." An active sentence with an evasive subject can still conceal responsibility. Keep a passive that preserves the topic or connects known information to new information, or when the actor is unknown or irrelevant (`G.active-voice`). Ask for author judgment when naming the actor would require a guess.
- **Make sentences easy to follow on first reading** (`E.sentence-flow`). Keep the main subject and verb close enough that the reader can follow their connection. Reduce nested clauses and interruptions when they delay the point. Introduce information in an order that connects it to what the reader already knows. Use short sentences for direct points and longer ones for relationships that belong together. Keep useful conjunctions and qualifications, and vary rhythm when that improves the passage. This layer sets no word limit or required length variation. A clear long sentence needs no edit solely because of its length.
- **Edit the argument before polishing sentences** (`E.structure-and-pace`). Check what each paragraph contributes and whether the sequence supports the intended explanation or conclusion. Repair missing connections before changing individual words. Develop one point per paragraph, with the evidence and qualification it needs. Give difficult reasoning enough explanation or an example, and shorten routine background that delays the point. Use headings, tables, or figures when they make the material easier to follow. Preserve the structure required by the genre, including research questions, methods, results, and limitations in a paper. Do not invent anecdotes or impose a news-story opening on an academic article.
- **Remove words that add no meaning** (`E.concision`). Delete repetition, empty lead-ins, redundant modifiers, and unnecessary nominalizations when the shorter version preserves meaning and flow. Prefer a positive statement to a tangle of negatives when the two are equivalent. "The logs do not exclude a timeout" cannot become "The logs show a timeout." Keep words that distinguish scope, uncertainty, time, or logical relations, including articles and clause markers. Stop shortening when further deletion would make the reader reconstruct missing information (`G.context-first`).

## Tone and imagery

- **Persuade with evidence and a civil tone** (`E.reasoned-tone`). Explain why a conclusion follows instead of asserting that it is obvious or belittling those who disagree. Present a relevant objection fairly and address its strongest supported form. Avoid false balance when the evidence favors one conclusion. Remove self-congratulation, promotional superlatives, and routine intensifiers when facts can express the point. Keep uncertainty warranted by the evidence, and distinguish an allegation from an established fact. State harm plainly without euphemism or exaggeration, and describe people with respect.
- **Use imagery to clarify a specific relation** (`E.imagery`). Inspect stock metaphors, journalistic formulas, borrowed scientific terms, and cultural allusions for what they actually communicate. Replace them when they are stale, misleading, or require knowledge that the audience may not share. Keep an apt image only within the limits of `G.no-figurative-language`, with an explanation of the comparison where needed. Use scientific terms in their technical sense when that sense is relevant. Do not add puns, fresh metaphors, or a conspicuous magazine voice merely to imitate The Economist.

## Numbers

- **Give numbers a basis for comparison** (`E.number-context`). Supply the unit, denominator, baseline, and time period needed to understand a number. Explain scale with a relevant comparison, and compare equivalent measures over compatible periods. Specify the kind of average when it affects interpretation. Distinguish a relative percentage change from a percentage-point change. A rise from 12% to 18% is six percentage points, or a 50% relative increase. Give the absolute baseline for a relative risk when available. If essential context is missing, follow `E.keep-original` rather than guessing.
- **Match numerical detail to its purpose** (`E.number-precision`). Select the figures that support the point and explain what they show. Put a dense set of results in a table when that helps readers compare them. Round or use an approximate fraction only when the approximation is marked and the lost precision is unimportant to the task. Preserve exact measurements, uncertainty, sample sizes, statistical results, and reproduction details where they are needed (`S.exact-p-values`, `S.effect-sizes`). A significance test alone establishes neither causation nor practical importance. This layer imposes no quota for numbers per paragraph and no automatic rounding rule.

## Editorial judgment

- **Keep the best version of the author's argument** (`E.keep-original`). Identify the concrete problem and explain how the proposed change solves it. Preserve purpose, evidence, distinctions, emphasis, and voice. Leave effective prose alone when the alternative is merely a personal preference. When an improvement depends on missing facts or an unresolved interpretation, retain the uncertain passage. In review reports, list a focused question under **Items requiring author judgment**. In other tasks, raise a brief query outside the document only if the missing information prevents completion. Do not supply a speculative replacement. Reread edited passages for continuity and check headings, captions, quotations, and numbers as well as body text.

## Self-check before presenting text (Economist)

1. **Scope and conventions** (`E.scope`, `E.precedence`). Apply the layer only within the requested scope. Preserve literal material and the selected project, research, and STE conventions.
2. **Reader knowledge** (`E.reader-knowledge`). Explain unfamiliar roles and necessary terms for the intended audience. Remove needless acronyms without changing established terminology.
3. **Word choice** (`E.precise-words`). Prefer a familiar equivalent where it preserves meaning. Keep necessary technical and conceptual distinctions.
4. **Actors and actions** (`E.actors-and-actions`). Use only actors established by the source, and preserve a passive that suits the passage.
5. **Sentence flow** (`E.sentence-flow`). Check nested clauses, subject-verb distance, information order, and rhythm. Revise a sentence for a reading problem, not its word count alone.
6. **Structure and pace** (`E.structure-and-pace`). Check the contribution and order of paragraphs before polishing wording. Give difficult steps enough explanation and preserve the genre.
7. **Concision** (`E.concision`). Remove empty or repeated material while keeping qualifications, clause markers, and connections that aid understanding.
8. **Tone** (`E.reasoned-tone`). Support conclusions, treat objections fairly, and replace evasive or inflated language with accurate statements.
9. **Imagery** (`E.imagery`). Check formulas and comparisons for a clear purpose. Keep imagery within the general and selected STE limits.
10. **Numerical context** (`E.number-context`). Check units, baselines, periods, averages, and comparability. Distinguish percentage changes from percentage-point changes.
11. **Numerical precision** (`E.number-precision`). Preserve required detail and uncertainty. Mark approximations, and distinguish statistical evidence from causation and practical importance.
12. **Editing judgment** (`E.keep-original`). Verify that each revision solves a demonstrated problem and preserves the argument. In reviews, list essential unresolved questions for the author. In other tasks, place necessary queries outside the document. Reread the edited passage.
