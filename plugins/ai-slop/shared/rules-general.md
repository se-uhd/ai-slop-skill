# Writing rules: general layer

This file is the base layer of the writing rules. It applies to any prose, scientific
or not, in any format. Other layers build on it. `rules-scientific.md` adds
conventions for empirical research articles, and `rules-latex.md` adds mechanics
for LaTeX source. The optional `rules-economist.md` adds core advice from
*Writing with Style: The Economist Guide*, and `rules-ste.md` adds rules based on
Simplified Technical English. A skill loads the layers that its scope calls for
(general only; general + scientific; or general + scientific + LaTeX), plus the
Economist layer with `--economist` and the STE layer with `--ste`.

These rules target AI slop and the habits that make machine-written prose
recognizable, plus universal points of clean writing. The general AI trope
catalog (named patterns such as negative parallelism, em-dash addiction, and
rule-of-three groupings, each tagged with a status) is fetched separately from
tropes.fyi. This layer carries the table of restricted words with alternatives
and a self-check. The rationale behind the rules that a reader might push back on
is in `rules-rationale.md`, which the skills do not load. Mechanical rules
carry their own short justification or none.

## Apply the rules in context

- **Judge the passage before changing it** (`G.context-first`). Apply the stylistic rules as diagnostics, not as automatic replacements. Read the surrounding paragraph and identify what the sentence contributes before proposing an edit. A word match, a trope label, or a count above a threshold is a reason to inspect, not proof of a defect. A stylistic finding needs a concrete explanation of what is unclear, misleading, inflated, redundant, or obstructive, and why the replacement improves it. Preserve the purpose of the sentence, its logical relations, distinctions, emphasis, scope, evidential strength, and the author's voice. An abstraction can be the right level of description, a familiar metaphor can clarify a relation, and a transition can do argumentative work. Do not replace a statement of purpose with a summary of topics, unpack a summary that the following sentences already explain, or remove a contrast between competing claims. Prefer the smallest change that fixes the demonstrated problem, and leave the original when the alternative is merely shorter, more literal, or more explicit without a clear benefit. This principle governs every stylistic rule and self-check in this and the other layers, and the trope catalog. Explicit user choices and concrete conventions such as spelling, terminology, citation syntax, and statistical reporting still apply. Report a convention correction as a convention, not as a claim that the prose is unclear or machine-written.

## Language

- **Use American English consistently** (`G.american-english`). That means analyze (not analyse), behavior (not behaviour), organization (not organisation), modeling (not modelling), color (not colour), favor (not favour), center (not centre), defense (not defence), and license (not licence). Check for British spellings introduced by collaborators or spell-checkers set to the wrong locale.
- **Treat "data" as singular** (`G.data-singular`). "The data shows" and "the data is," not "the data show" or "the data are." Style guides accept both forms, and this project uses the singular consistently.
- **Use "such as" not "like" to introduce examples** (`G.such-as`). Write "scenarios such as code completion," not "scenarios like code completion." `Like` is colloquial in this construction, and a rewrite replaces it even when the original uses it. `Like` remains correct as a verb ("readers like terse prose") and in deliberate similes.

## Restricted words (use sparingly)

**Restricted words** (`G.restricted-words`). Legitimate words that AI overuses. Each use should be intentional. If a simpler word works, use it. More than 2 to 3 restricted words in a single paragraph prompts a closer look, not an automatic rewrite. Keep a word that is apt in context or part of an established term.

| Restricted | Preferred alternative(s) | When the restricted form is acceptable |
|---|---|---|
| utilize | use | Only when the distinction from "use" matters |
| leverage | use, apply, employ | Only when the lever metaphor is intentional |
| facilitate | enable, support, help, allow | n/a |
| foster | encourage, support, promote | n/a |
| streamline | simplify, reduce, speed up | Acceptable in process-improvement contexts |
| underscore | emphasize, highlight, stress | n/a |
| harness | use, apply, employ | n/a |
| encompass | include, cover, span | n/a |
| navigate | handle, manage, deal with | Only for literal movement through a UI, website, menu, or file tree, never as a metaphor for handling difficulty ("navigate the challenges" -> "handle the challenges") |
| landscape | field, area, environment, situation | n/a |
| nuanced | detailed, subtle, qualified | n/a |
| multifaceted | complex, varied, many-sided | n/a |
| intricate | complex, detailed, elaborate | n/a |
| meticulous | careful, thorough, rigorous | n/a |
| transformative | n/a | Only when genuinely describing transformation |

**Standard vocabulary (e.g., novel, robust, comprehensive, insights, mitigate, enhance, innovative, paradigm, framework, stakeholder, fundamentally, inherently)** and transition words (e.g., moreover, furthermore, notably, conversely, crucially) are not restricted. Prefer a simpler word when it works equally well, and when more than two transition words appear in one paragraph, check whether any can go without losing the logical flow. The connectives that mark a consequence need the further check below, because AI text uses one of them for nearly every consequence.

**Consequence connectives** (`G.consequence-connectives`). AI text uses one word for nearly every consequence: "so" in general prose, "therefore" and "thus" in academic prose. The concentration, not any single use, is the tell, whichever word a document uses most. Delete a sentence-initial "So" that is an empty spoken opener ("So, the counts differ because the parser skips blank lines", see **No formulaic section openings** (`G.no-formulaic-openings`)), and keep one that marks a needed consequence in the register of the document. Sentence-initial "Therefore," and "Thus," can mark a conclusion that follows from the preceding sentence, and mid-position ("The counts are therefore unreliable") is worth considering when it reads more naturally. Between clauses, delete the connective only when the relation remains clear without it and without extra effort, and keep one that distinguishes a consequence from mere sequence or addition even above the density signal. Where one is needed, match it to the step: "so" for a small step in plain prose, therefore or hence for a conclusion drawn from evidence just given, thus for a consequence that follows by construction, and consequently or as a result for an outcome that the text then discusses. Where the cause reads better after the effect, recast with because or since. Substitute one connective for another last, and count the substitutes toward the transition-word limit above. Count the family together. More than two or three per page-equivalent (~350 words) of running prose flags a text that signposts every step, and at that density one word carrying nearly all of them shows the habit, as does a run of consecutive sentences with the same connective. Parallelism across items of the same kind stays, as in one sentence per exit code. This rule governs the consequence relation, and **Name the relation with a connective** (`G.connectives`) governs example and restatement. The intensifier ("so few cases survived"), the purpose connective ("so that a reviewer can check the quote"), the correlative ("so large that"), and quoted material do not count.

**Precedence over the trope catalog** (`G.catalog-precedence`). The catalog names patterns rather than a closed word list, and several entries overlap with these rules. Its **"Delve" and friends** entry names "robust" in one family with "utilize", "leverage", "streamline", and "harness", where this layer leaves robust unrestricted and lists the other four with alternatives. Its **Signposted conclusion** entry flags every "In summary" and "In conclusion", which **No formulaic closings** (`G.no-formulaic-closings`) permits in a genuine summary position. Its **Rule of Three pattern** and **Bold-first bullets** entries overlap **No rule-of-three defaults** (`G.no-rule-of-three`) and **No excessive bold or formatting** (`G.no-excess-bold`), which carry the exceptions. Where the catalog and a layer rule disagree, the layer rule wins. The catalog supplies candidates to inspect, and the rules state the deliberate exceptions. Apply `G.context-first` to every candidate, whatever its catalog status. A frequency rating prioritizes inspection and does not establish a defect in a passage.

## Phrases to avoid

**Phrases to avoid** (`G.phrases-to-avoid`). Stock formulations that read as AI filler or stilted register. Replace each with the plain alternative. The trope catalog covers the broader set of named patterns, and this list holds the additions of this layer.

- **"worked example(s)"** -> "example(s)". The qualifier rarely adds information. Keep it only for a fully solved problem walked through step by step, where the contrast with an unsolved exercise is the point.
- **"earn its keep" / "earn its place" / "pull its weight"** -> state what the element contributes, as in "the opener earns its keep" -> "the opener introduces the list that follows".
- **"headline numbers" / "headline figures"** -> name the figures plainly ("the largest claims", "the most-cited figures", or "the figures"). "Headline" editorializes the numbers in a journalistic register.
- **"arm" for a condition, group, variant, or split** -> "condition" or "treatment" for an experimental manipulation, "group" for a set of subjects, "variant" or "configuration" for a version of a prompt, model, or pipeline, and "split" for a data partition. Keep "arm" for a randomized trial group or a bandit option only.
- **"famously" / "notoriously" / "as is well known" / "a classic"** -> state the claim and its source, or drop the appeal, as in "the tool is famously slow" -> "the tool took 40 minutes per build in our benchmark". The phrase borrows consensus without evidence and marks the claim that most needs a citation.

## Terminology consistency

- **Use one term for one concept** (`G.one-term`). Once you introduce a term for a concept, use that same term throughout the document, and do not alternate between synonyms for variety. What reads as elegant variation in literary writing creates ambiguity in technical writing. If "code review" is the term, do not switch to "code inspection" or "review process" later.
- **Define terms on first use, then reuse exactly** (`G.define-once`). If a concept needs a definition or explanation, provide it once. After that, the established term carries the meaning without re-explanation.
- **Synonyms are acceptable only for genuinely different concepts** (`G.distinct-synonyms`). If two terms refer to distinct concepts, use both, but make the distinction explicit.

## Reference

- **Anchor sentence-initial pronouns** (`G.anchor-pronouns`). Name the antecedent of a sentence-initial pronoun (*This*, *These*, *That*, *It*) when competing readings, distance, or a change of topic makes it unclear, as in "The compiler emits a warning when the cache is stale. It is disabled by default." -> "The warning is disabled by default." A clause-sized antecedent can take a head noun when that makes the connection clearer ("The parser accepts unterminated strings. This causes silent data loss." -> "This leniency causes silent data loss."). A pronoun that clearly refers to the immediately preceding claim stays. Do not force noun repetition solely because a sentence begins with one. Exempt: dummy *it* ("it is unclear whether") and a demonstrative that carries its noun ("this study").
- **A summarizing noun must name something that the text has listed** (`G.summarizing-nouns`). *Such tools*, *this approach*, or *these challenges* after a passage that names no tools, approach, or challenges refers to a category that the text never introduced. Use the noun for what was listed, or introduce the category, as in "LLMs can generate unit tests, triage bug reports, and summarize pull requests. Such tools are already in industrial use." -> "Tools for these tasks are already in industrial use."
- **Definite article only for known referents** (`G.definite-article`). A first mention takes *a/an*, the zero article for mass and plural nouns, or an explicit introduction. *The* is for something already mentioned, unique in context, or entailed by something mentioned. Ask "which one?", and drop the definite when the text so far cannot answer, as in "He built a test generator and the rerun infrastructure to detect flaky tests." -> "He built a test generator and infrastructure to rerun the generated tests and detect which are flaky." A purpose or description after the noun ("the tooling to support X") does not by itself identify it. Exempt: postmodifiers that single out one referent ("the version we used"), nouns unique by nature or convention ("the compiler"), and generic definites ("the developer sees a warning").
- **Keep stand-in references clear** (`G.stand-ins`). A word that stands for a noun or clause (*ones*, *those*, *the former / the latter*, *the same*, *respectively*, *do so*) can make the reader search for its referent. Replace it when the immediate context leaves competing readings or requires a burdensome lookup, as in "detect flaky tests among the generated ones" -> "detect which of the generated tests are flaky" and "precision and recall were 0.81 and 0.64, respectively" -> "precision was 0.81 and recall 0.64". Keep a clear reference when spelling it out adds repetition without improving comprehension. A reference to the preceding clause or sentence is not a defect merely because its noun could be repeated.

## Voice

- **Prefer active voice** (`G.active-voice`). Use passive only when the actor is unknown or irrelevant, or when the passive reads better. As a secondary signal, a paragraph in which more than one-third of the sentences are passive is worth a second pass.
- **Use consistent tense within and across adjacent paragraphs** (`G.consistent-tense`). Unmotivated tense shifts are distracting. Switch tense only when the temporal frame changes.
- **Participial openings** (`G.participial-openings`). Repeated present-participle openings ("-ing, ...") can make prose monotonous or obscure who acts. More than one per page is a prompt to inspect their effect, not a quota. Keep an opening that expresses the relation clearly, and revise repetitions that impede the passage.

## Clause boundaries

- **Keep *that* in formal prose** (`G.keep-that`). Omitting the relativizer of a restrictive object relative ("the metrics the benchmark reports") or the complementizer after a reporting verb ("the results show the effect persists") is grammatical but conversational, and AI text drops it. Scan for two noun phrases in a row with nothing between them, and for a verb that can take a direct object (e.g., show, find, confirm, report, suggest, indicate, ensure, mean, argue, conclude) followed by a noun phrase that turns out to be a clause subject, which the reader first parses as the object. Restore *that*, or *who* for people, as in "the metrics that the benchmark reports" and "the results show that the effect persists." Exempt: fixed heads ("the way we", "the reason we"), quoted text, and deliberately informal text (commit messages, code comments). Non-restrictive *which* needs no check.
- **Rewrite *whose* clauses on things** (`G.whose-on-things`). AI text states a condition on a property of a thing as a restrictive *whose* clause. Restate it with *with* ("a model whose weights are public" -> "a model with public weights"), with a participle or verb on the head ("entries whose keys appear in the new citations" -> "entries cited in the new text"), or with the property as the subject of its own clause ("skip a finding whose quote cannot be located" -> "skip a finding if its quote cannot be located"), when the restatement loses nothing. Keep *whose* for a person or group ("authors whose papers were retracted") and where every restatement is longer or repeats the head noun. More than one per page-equivalent (~350 words) of running prose is a secondary signal.
- **Possessive 's on things** (`G.possessive-on-things`). AI text puts 's on things and abstractions ("the sentence's purpose", "the repository's commit messages"), which states a part, an attribute, or content as if it were ownership. Prefer the construction that names the relation: an of-phrase ("the purpose of the sentence"), a preposition ("the commit messages in the repository"), or a noun adjunct where no misread arises ("the report's name" -> "the report name"). Keep 's for a person, group, organization, or named tool ("the author's voice", "DBLP's API"), for a document standing for its authors ("the paper's claim"), in fixed phrases ("today's date", "a master's thesis"), and where the possessor is the topic under discussion and the of-phrase would move it to the end. A count is no signal, since one 's per paragraph is ordinary. Inspect a head noun that takes 's throughout a document and never the of-form, and any phrase with two possessives ("the report's finding's quote").

## Punctuation

- **Em-dashes** (`G.em-dashes`). Judge each dash. Reserve it for a genuine interruption or an appositive that needs setting off, and where a period or comma reads better, use that. Never swap an em-dash for parentheses, since both signal a sentence with too many ideas. Count is a secondary signal. More than 2 to 3 per page-equivalent (~350 words) of running prose flags over-reliance, but one dash used wrongly is a worse problem than three used well. Two structural uses are legitimate and do not count toward the signal: an appositive that already contains commas (`Three reviewers — Alice, Bob, and Carol — flagged the issue`) and a parenthetical that itself contains parentheses (`the corpus — built from public repositories (GitHub, GitLab) — covers ...`). Em-dashes inside quoted material belong to the quotation and do not count, and neither do glyphs that are not em-dashes, such as en dashes in ranges (`pp. 12–18`), minus signs, and hyphens or `--` in code listings.
- **Literal em-dash glyphs in source** (`G.em-dash-glyphs`). A literal `—` (U+2014) in code-edited source is a tell on its own, because editors do not autocorrect `-` or `--` into `—`, so humans type `-`, `--`, or `:` (and `---` in LaTeX). Flag every literal `—` in Markdown, plain text, comments, or `.tex`, and replace it with the ASCII form of the format. A `—` inside a quotation or a code listing stays. The common case is a uniform `term — gloss` list separator (e.g., an API reference or a glossary). Keep the list, and prefer `term: gloss`, a hyphen, or a table. This rule is about the glyph, and converting it is not by itself a fix for the dash. The per-mark test and the density signal of **Em-dashes** (`G.em-dashes`) apply to any mark used in place of a dash (`--`, a spaced hyphen, LaTeX's `---`) as to `—`.
- **Colons** (`G.colons`). Judge each colon. It belongs before a list, a definition, or a deliberate setup-and-payoff that justifies the pause, not as a generic mid-sentence pause or in place of an em-dash or parentheses that a rule forbade. Use a period when it clarifies the relation without losing the setup or emphasis. Count is a secondary signal. More than 2 per page-equivalent in running prose, or a colon followed by a list in every other paragraph, flags over-reliance. Headings, table captions, and `figure:`-style labels are exempt.
- **Colon before a list or continuation** (`G.introducer-colon`). When a sentence introduces an enumerated list, a numbered structure (e.g., `(1) ... (2) ... (3) ...`), or a paragraph that grammatically completes or elaborates it, end the sentence with `:`, not the `.` that AI text defaults to. If what follows stands as an independent sentence on its own topic, the period is correct. Such introducer colons do not count toward the colon signal, which targets mid-sentence colons used as filler pauses, em-dash substitutes, or decorative setup.
- **Capitalization after a colon** (`G.colon-capitalization`). In running prose, capitalize the first word after a colon when a complete sentence follows ("We adopt one rule: Capitalize after a colon when a full sentence follows."), and keep it lowercase before a fragment, phrase, single word, or list ("We considered three options: speed, accuracy, and cost."). The capitalization is a project convention, and AI prose defaults to lowercase whether or not a sentence follows. Headings and `figure:` / `table:` labels are exempt. A colon that introduces an enumerated series of independent clauses is a list, so its first word stays lowercase. Splitting three or more such clauses into sentences (see **No list-cramming in a single sentence** (`G.no-list-cramming`)) removes the question.
- **Semicolons** (`G.semicolons`). Judge each semicolon. It joins two closely related independent clauses where the link adds meaning that a period would lose, or separates items in a list where commas would be ambiguous. It is not a generic pause or a default substitute for a period. If a period loses nothing, use the period. The most common misuse is a semicolon between two independent clauses, often before a clause that opens with a pronoun (e.g., we, it, this, they). Default to a period there. Count is a secondary signal. More than 1 to 2 per page-equivalent in running prose flags over-reliance.
- **Name the relation with a connective** (`G.connectives`). When a flagged em-dash or colon introduces an example or a restatement, the precise fix is usually the connective that names that relation, not a bare period or comma: an example connective (such as, e.g., for example, including) when what follows illustrates, and a restatement connective (i.e., that is, in other words, namely) when it renames, defines, or restates, as in "Some checks run before the build: linting and type checking." -> "Some checks, such as linting and type checking, run before the build." and "The parser is strict — it rejects any unknown key." -> "The parser is strict, that is, it rejects any unknown key." These connectives are standard in academic prose and under-used by AI text. Apply the fix only when the mark genuinely introduces an example or a restatement. A dash or colon that sets up a single payoff clause (see **Colons** (`G.colons`)) or introduces a true list is neither and stays. Pick one example signal (`e.g.,` or `such as`) and stay with it, and never pair `e.g.,` with a trailing `etc.` (see **Mark parenthetical lists as examples or exhaustive** (`G.parenthetical-lists`) and **One example signal per document** (`G.one-example-signal`)).
- **Combined pause-mark count** (`G.pause-mark-count`). Em-dashes, colons, and semicolons all create mid-sentence pauses, and AI text shifts the load between them when one mark is restricted, so judging each mark alone misses the pattern. The primary test stays per mark. As a combined secondary signal, a page near all three individual ceilings at once (say 3 em-dashes, 2 colons, 2 semicolons) is over-punctuated even though no single ceiling is exceeded. Around 5 or fewer per page-equivalent of running prose is a reasonable target, and for each mark, ask whether a split into two sentences reads better.
- **Sentence length** (`G.sentence-length`). Let sentence length follow the argument and vary where that helps the reader. Three consecutive sentences within 5 words of each other are a candidate for checking rhythm, not a requirement to revise one. Keep useful parallelism and sentences that already read naturally. Do not split or lengthen a sentence solely to change a metric, or homogenize sentence length when editing.
- **Hyphenation of compound modifiers** (`G.compound-hyphens`). AI text over-hyphenates noun-noun stacks placed before another noun ("code-generation benchmarks", "data-collection process"). Such stacks are usually unambiguous without the hyphen ("code generation benchmarks"), so drop it. Keep the hyphen only when the unhyphenated form invites a real misread, typically when one element is a participle that could re-attach as a verb ("instruction-following ability" stays, because "instruction following ability" briefly reads with "following" as a verb). Genuine compound adjectives with an adjective or participle (e.g., high-quality, well-known, large-scale) keep the hyphen. An invented compound such as "review-flattening" is a coinage to rewrite, not a hyphen to drop (see **No invented compounds or verbs-as-nouns** (`G.no-coinages`)).
- **Oxford comma** (`G.oxford-comma`). Use it, but do not let comma-heavy constructions substitute for clearer sentence structure.

## Structure

- **Build connected prose** (`G.connected-prose`). Develop a line of argument that readers can follow across sentences and paragraphs. Connect new information to what the reader knows, and make clear how each paragraph advances the question or purpose of the section. Explain the substantive relation between points, such as how a limitation motivates a method or how evidence qualifies a claim. A succession of statements joined only by "then", "next", or "also" can leave that relation unexplained, so repair the missing connection rather than vary the transition words. Keep conjunctions, subordinate clauses, clear references, and brief transitions that provide continuity, even when a shorter version is possible, and do not turn a paragraph into isolated subject-verb sentences to satisfy a punctuation or concision rule. Let the subject and genre determine the storyline, and express only relations that the text supports. Chronology and numbered steps suit procedures and accounts where order matters. After local edits, reread the passage for continuity and progression.
- **No formulaic section openings** (`G.no-formulaic-openings`). Never open a section or subsection with "In today's...", "In the realm of...", "In an era of...", "As we explore...", or "[Topic] has become increasingly...", with an announcement of what follows ("Two constraints shape the design.", "The more important point is where the decision sits."), or with a paraphrase of the question being answered. Open with the substance, as in "Two constraints shape the design. The first is latency." -> "The design has to keep latency under 50 ms."
- **No formulaic closings** (`G.no-formulaic-closings`). Do not close a paragraph with "Overall, ...", "In summary, ...", or "In conclusion, ..." restating what it already said. "In summary" and "In conclusion" are acceptable in actual summary or conclusion sections, "Overall" when synthesizing across data points or conditions ("Overall, the pattern held across all three groups"), and "In summary, ..." before an enumerated list that consolidates several preceding paragraphs into a checklist ("In summary, our recommendation is to report: (1) X; (2) Y; (3) Z."). The diagnostic is whether the phrase consolidates content that the reader has not yet seen in list form. If the next construct is a numbered or bulleted list distilling earlier prose, the opener is justified. If it introduces a sentence or two that just restates the paragraph above, it is filler.
- **Paragraph length** (`G.paragraph-length`). In sustained running prose, a paragraph normally has more than two sentences and develops one point with the explanation, evidence, or qualification that it needs. A one- or two-sentence paragraph is an exception with a clear structural purpose, such as a section transition, an introduction to a list or figure, or a self-contained qualification that merging would obscure. Combine related sentences that were split across short paragraphs, preserving their connections (`G.connected-prose`). A sentence fragment set as its own paragraph ("Flaky tests. A twenty-minute build." -> "The tests are flaky, and the build takes twenty minutes.") is manufactured emphasis and qualifies for no exception. Inspect a page with more than one or two one- or two-sentence paragraphs, or any run of three or more, for repeated fragmentation. Develop an incomplete point from information already in the text, and do not add filler, split sentences artificially, or combine unrelated topics to reach a count. List items, captions, and complete short replies follow their own format.
- **No rule-of-three defaults** (`G.no-rule-of-three`). Do not reflexively group items in threes. If there are two items, list two. If there are four, list four.
- **Announce a count only when the reader tracks it** (`G.no-announced-counts`). Do not state how many items follow when what follows is continuous prose ("There are three reasons for this.", "We discuss five aspects of the design."). Delete the number and state the first item, as in "There are three reasons for this. The first is cost." -> "Cost is one reason." Keep the count where the reader has to check each item against it: an enumerated specification or checklist; the conditions, phases, or research questions of a study; and the contribution list that a venue expects. The test is a later sentence that refers back to the number ("the third of these", "both of these constraints"). If none does, the number is decoration. This rule governs whether the count is announced, and **No rule-of-three defaults** (`G.no-rule-of-three`) how many items there are.
- **Keep enumerations short** (`G.short-enumerations`). Examples introduced by "e.g.,", "such as", "including", or numbered markers should illustrate, not exhaustively iterate. Two or three representative items are almost always enough. If the full set matters, use a table, figure, or dedicated list rather than a parenthetical or running sentence. When in doubt, cut the enumeration to the clearest examples and stop.
- **No list-cramming in a single sentence** (`G.no-list-cramming`). Do not flatten three or more independent clauses into one sentence as a run-in series, typically a colon or dash opening a chain of semicolon-joined clauses, each often with its own citation or parenthetical, so that one sentence carries what should be several. Break the chain into sentences that make the relations among the claims clear (`G.connected-prose`), using `First, ... Second, ...` only when the order or numbering helps the reader follow actual stages or numbered items. Only when the items reduce to short parallel fragments, keep the colon so it introduces a true list ("three options: speed, accuracy, and cost"). Two tightly linked clauses joined by a semicolon, and a colon or dash introducing a single payoff clause, are fine. The target is a sentence that joins three or more clauses, not the punctuation mark, which distinguishes this rule from **Keep enumerations short** (`G.short-enumerations`) (illustrative-list length) and **No rule-of-three defaults** (`G.no-rule-of-three`) (item count).
- **No enumerated list disguised as paragraphs** (`G.no-prose-listicle`). Do not open consecutive paragraphs with ordinals that number an unmarked list ("The first wall is the absence of a scoped API... The second wall is the lack of delegated access..."). Either set the material as a real list, or drop the ordinal scaffolding, open each paragraph with its own claim ("The service offers no API with scoped access."), and make clear how the claims relate (`G.connected-prose`). In a research article, where the venue rules out a bulleted list, the second fix is the only one. Within a paragraph, keep ordinals that identify actual stages or numbered items, and do not add them merely to connect claims when splitting a sentence under `G.no-list-cramming`.
- **Mark parenthetical lists as examples or exhaustive** (`G.parenthetical-lists`). A bare parenthetical with two or more comma-separated items reads ambiguously, since the reader cannot tell whether the list is representative or complete. Open it with "e.g.," (examples) or "i.e.," (definition or full enumeration), or restructure, and when neither is clear and the list is not obviously exhaustive, use "e.g.,". An inline list in running text introduced by nothing takes "such as" or "including." Exempt: an abbreviation expansion ("large language models (LLMs)") and a parenthetical that names a single concept.
- **One example signal per document** (`G.one-example-signal`). Do not mix `e.g.,` with `etc.` in one parenthetical, since "(e.g., a, b, etc.)" is redundant. Write "(e.g., a, b)". Across a document, prefer `e.g., ...` at the start over `..., etc.` at the end and apply the choice consistently.
- **No excessive bold or formatting** (`G.no-excess-bold`). Do not bold phrases for emphasis in running text. Reserve bold for subsection-level headers or the existing conventions of the document. In a list, a bold lead-in is acceptable when it names the item, as the term being defined or the name of a rule does, and a tic when it bolds the opening words of each item, which is how most AI-written documentation formats its lists.
- **Refer back instead of repeating** (`G.refer-back`). State each definition, point, or finding once, where it belongs, and refer back to it from elsewhere. A one-sentence reminder is acceptable when needed to follow an argument. A paragraph of restatement is not.
- **Cut padding at the sentence level** (`G.sentence-padding`). A phrase can be concrete, plain, and unique yet spend more words than its content needs, which is distinct from vagueness (`G.be-concrete`), inflated vocabulary (`G.restricted-words`), and duplication (`G.refer-back`). Replace multi-word filler with its shorter equivalent ("in order to" -> "to", "due to the fact that" -> "because", "has the ability to" -> "can", "the majority of" -> "most"). Delete empty lead-ins ("it is important to note that", "as can be seen"). Cut redundant doublets ("end result", "future plans", "completely eliminate"). The diagnostic is subtractive. If deleting a word or clause costs no meaning, emphasis, precision, or continuity, delete it. A connective or reference that helps the reader follow the argument is not padding (`G.connected-prose`).
- **Cut padding at the paragraph level** (`G.paragraph-padding`). A paragraph makes its point in as many sentences as it needs and no more. Remove a windup sentence that announces what the same paragraph then does, and a sentence that restates its predecessor without adding a claim, qualification, evidence, or a needed connection, but keep a transition that explains how the paragraph follows from the preceding one (`G.connected-prose`). A paragraph that opens a section is not windup when it states the claim or purpose of the section or orients the reader across the subsections with something that the headings do not state (e.g., the order, what the parts share, a definition they all need). It is windup when it merely lists the subsection headings or repeats content stated elsewhere (`G.refer-back`). A paragraph that takes five sentences to deliver one sentence of content is padded even when every sentence is clean. Padding is the opposite failure from **Paragraph length** (`G.paragraph-length`), which guards against paragraphs cut too short for manufactured emphasis. Generic-truth sentences, true of any document on the subject ("Software testing is a critical part of software development"), aphorisms written to be quoted rather than to advance the argument ("Every metric that rewards volume punishes leverage"), and evaluative sentences that grade the preceding claim without adding to it ("This is an important finding") are legitimate in small numbers and filler in quantity. One such sentence can open a section or mark that a result matters. Flag a paragraph with more than one, or a page-equivalent (~350 words) with more than two or three. Where the paragraph already states the claim, fold the grade into it, as in "Build times doubled. This is an important finding." -> "Build times doubled, the largest change that we measured."
- **Reformulate, do not delete** (`G.reformulate`). The default fix for flagged prose is a rewrite into a clear, correct form that keeps the author's content. Delete only genuine filler (the padding, windup sentences, and unsupported hedges that the rules above target) or what the author explicitly asks to cut. A flagged claim, example, or qualification carries information that the author chose to include, and deleting it discards that information silently.

## Tone

- **Match the voice of the existing document** (`G.match-voice`). Read the draft before writing. Mirror its register, its level of formality, and how it handles transitions.
- **Be concrete** (`G.be-concrete`). A generic head noun (thing, stuff, aspect, element, factor, area, item, component, piece, part, issue, concept, entity), sometimes behind a vague quantifier (a number of, various, certain, several), needs attention when the reader needs a specific referent and the context does not establish one. An abstract noun is not an empty placeholder merely because it names a category. Keep abstractions that state the purpose, scope, theoretical framing, or relation between claims, and summaries that the surrounding sentences explain. To test one, write the specific noun in its slot ("we changed three things" -> "we changed the retry interval and the cache key"). Where the text cannot supply that noun, the blank is the finding. Record it under author judgment with the candidate readings and leave the sentence alone, because a guessed noun states something that the author did not. An empty verb phrase takes the same test ("provides valuable insights into test flakiness" -> "shows that most flaky tests depend on timing"). In a description of a revision, do not use "moved" or "shifted" as vague substitutes for the change. Name the operation or the resulting behavior (a file was renamed, a default changed, a requirement was removed), as in "the requirements shifted in v2" -> "v2 requires Python 3.14", when the source establishes it, and never infer a specific change from vague wording. Describe current behavior without version history unless the history helps the reader compare versions, upgrade, reproduce a result, or understand a change. Keep "moved" for an actual relocation with a named source and destination ("the configuration file moved from the project root to config/"), and keep physical movement and established technical senses such as a bit shift. Keep the generic noun where the generality is the claim: a contrast with another category ("a noun that names a thing rather than an action"), an established technical sense (an XML element, a factor in factor analysis), or a category that the text has defined. A generic noun pointing back at a passage that listed nothing falls under **A summarizing noun must name something that the text has listed** (`G.summarizing-nouns`). No density signal applies, because the same words are ordinary technical vocabulary.
- **Hedge only from evidence** (`G.hedge-from-evidence`). "The data suggests X" is appropriate when the data is ambiguous. "One could argue that X" is filler unless the text says who argues it and why. As a secondary signal, more than two hedges in one paragraph (e.g., may, might, could, perhaps, to some extent) flag it. Keep the hedges that evidence supports and remove the rest.
- **Take positions** (`G.take-positions`). When the evidence points one direction, say so. Do not present artificial balance.
- **No author-voice metacommentary in published prose** (`G.no-metacommentary`). Asides such as "reader beware," "we shall see," "spoiler alert," or "(more on this below)" address the reader about the act of reading and break the formal register. Remove them from rendered body text, or move them into editorial notes that do not render, and treat them as a clear finding in body text, not a judgment call. A forward reference to a later section is fine as a plain pointer. The same rule covers sentences that narrate the reasoning behind the writing or announce that the writer is about to be careful or precise ("What that changes is worth being precise about.", "Before answering, it helps to separate two questions."). State the point instead.

## Plain, literal language

- **Use plain, literal language** (`G.plain-language`). The habits below make prose read as machine-written without adding meaning, and the fix for each is the plain version, with an exception for genuinely precise usage. Scan for the sample words that these rules name even when nothing reads wrong, since a reviewer with the writer's habit may not notice them.
  - **No invented compounds or verbs-as-nouns** (`G.no-coinages`). Do not join domain words into hyphenated modifiers used as if they were established terms ("review-flattening", "data-integrity gating"), and do not turn a verb into a noun ("the ask", "a full delete"), including the count nouns of systems jargon "a write", "a read", "a create", and "a delete" (as in "each write" or "reads and writes"). Rewrite to the verb or phrase that the coinage compressed: "review-flattening" -> "flattening the review", "data-integrity gating" -> "blocking changes that fail the integrity check", "the ask" -> "the request", "a full delete" -> "deleting all records", "a write" -> "a write request". The same pattern covers a noun phrase coined ad hoc to name a specific artifact, document, or table, hyphenated or not ("track-changes file", "data sources table"), which a description of the artifact replaces ("the latexdiff PDF", "a table listing all data sources"). Dropping the hyphen is not enough, and the participle exception of **Hyphenation of compound modifiers** (`G.compound-hyphens`) does not cover an invented gerund compound. The pattern also covers an abstract label built from a domain word and a problem-noun (e.g., paradox, trap, creep, divide, vacuum, inversion) and used as if established ("supervision paradox", "workload creep"). State the phenomenon in a clause instead ("workload creep" -> "the workload grew with each release"), or, when the text genuinely introduces a construct, define it on first use and keep the definition (`G.define-once`). Keep established terms and literal code, API, or config identifiers as written ("foreign-key constraint", a `user_secrets` key).
  - **Plain word over a colorful or insider synonym** (`G.plain-words`). Replace a vivid or niche word with its plain equivalent when the vivid word adds no precision: create not "mint", show or report not "surface", combine not "weave", guide not "shepherd", merge not "land", coordinate not "orchestrate", enable not "unlock", is in or is defined in not "lives in", structure, format, or pattern not the noun "shape", structure or influence not the verb "shape", and publish or create not "cut". Replace "shape" with the property that it stands for ("the shape of the catalog" -> "the structure of the catalog", "factors that shape adoption" -> "factors that influence adoption"), and "cut" with the act ("cut a release" -> "publish a release"). When "gate" or "checkpoint" vaguely stands for checks, name what must pass and, when it matters, before which action ("six commits, each ending at a green checkpoint" -> "six commits, and `gradle build` passes after each"), and choose wording that the context supports. Keep the word only for a distinction that the plain word loses: "exercise" for driving inputs through a specific code path, not for "run"; "seam" for a Feathers seam, not for any interface; "shape" for a geometric form, a statistical distribution, or the dimensions of an array, not for the structure of a document or a rule; "cut" for removing or shortening, not for creating or publishing; and "checkpoint" for a saved state that a process resumes from (e.g., a model checkpoint), not for a point at which checks pass. These words are a sample of an open pattern, not a closed list.
  - **Use figurative language with a clear purpose** (`G.no-figurative-language`). Replace idioms and clichés when they obscure or exaggerate the claim or repeat a stock formula without contributing meaning ("low-hanging fruit" -> "the easiest improvements", "the change did not move the needle" -> "the change did not lower the failure rate", "caching is a double-edged sword" -> "caching lowers latency but can serve stale data", "at the end of the day, the tests must pass" -> "the tests must pass"), and metaphorical framing when imagery substitutes for the relation that the reader needs ("the data tells a story of declining use" -> "the data shows that use declined", "X is the backbone of Y" -> "Y depends on X"). Familiar metaphors and conventional abstract framing stay when they communicate the relation or emphasis clearly. Do not personify. Personification predicates a mental state, a duty, or a merit of a subject that is not a person or a group: code, a model, a test, data, a document, a section, a sentence, a punctuation mark, a tool, a method, a rule, or an abstract noun. The finding is in the predicate, not the subject: a desire ("the model wants to" -> "the model is trained to"), a feeling ("the model struggles with long inputs" -> "accuracy drops on long inputs"), a belief or expectation ("the rule expects the reviewer to" -> "the rule requires that the reviewer"), or a duty or merit ("a period does the job" -> "a period is enough"; "earn its place" is under `G.phrases-to-avoid`). The fix names the mechanism or the observable behavior. A verb that names a defined behavior of the subject is literal (a function returns, a test fails, a parser expects a token, a query needs an index, a model learns), and so is the metonymy that names a document for its authors (the paper argues, Section 3 shows). Established technical terms with a metaphorical etymology are literal domain vocabulary (e.g., tree, branch, thread, pipeline, deadlock, mock), and one analogy that helps a reader understand an unfamiliar concept is fine when introduced once, marked as an analogy, and not continued through the text.

## Self-check before presenting text

Before presenting any written or edited text, apply **Judge the passage before changing it** (`G.context-first`) to these checks. They identify candidates for inspection, not a target number of edits:

1. **Word-level scan** (`G.restricted-words`, `G.catalog-precedence`). Scan for the word-level patterns in the catalog (**"Delve" and friends**, **"Tapestry" and "Landscape"**, **"Quietly" and other magic adverbs**) and assess their function in context before proposing a replacement, keeping vocabulary that these rules explicitly leave unrestricted (see **Precedence over the trope catalog** (`G.catalog-precedence`)). Count restricted words (per the table above). If more than 2 to 3 appear in a single paragraph, inspect whether they obscure or inflate the claim. Do not replace an apt word merely to reduce the count.
2. **Em-dashes** (`G.em-dashes`). Check each em-dash. Replace it where a period or comma would read better. As a secondary signal, more than 2 to 3 per page-equivalent (~350 words) of running prose flags over-reliance. Exclude from any count: em-dashes inside quoted source material, and glyphs that are not em-dashes at all, such as en dashes in numeric or page ranges (`pp. 12–18`), minus signs in math, and hyphens or `--` sequences inside code listings. Also exclude the two structural exceptions noted in the **Em-dashes** (`G.em-dashes`) rule (appositives that contain commas, and parentheticals that contain parentheses).
3. **Colons** (`G.colons`). Examine each colon. Use a period where it clarifies the relation without losing the setup or emphasis. Watch especially for colons substituting for em-dashes or parentheses, and for a colon followed by a list in paragraph after paragraph. As a secondary signal, more than 2 per page-equivalent in running prose flags over-reliance.
4. **Introducer punctuation** (`G.introducer-colon`). For every sentence immediately followed by an enumerated list, a numbered structure (e.g., `(1) ... (2) ... (3) ...`), a bulleted block, or a paragraph that grammatically continues or elaborates it, verify that the introducer ends with `:`, not `.`. AI text systematically defaults to `.` here. The `:` is correct and does not count toward the colon signal.
5. **Capitalization after a colon** (`G.colon-capitalization`). For every colon in running prose, check whether the text that follows is a complete sentence. If it is, the first word must be capitalized. If it is a fragment, phrase, single word, or list, keep the first word lowercase. Fix mismatches. For a colon introducing an enumerated series of independent clauses, keep the first word lowercase (it is a list) and prefer splitting three or more such clauses into sentences (see `G.no-list-cramming`).
6. **Semicolons** (`G.semicolons`). Examine each semicolon. Consider a period when it makes the relation easier to read. The fact that both clauses can stand alone is not enough to require a change. Reserve semicolons for genuine list disambiguation and for tightly coupled independent clauses where the link adds meaning that a period would lose. As a secondary signal, more than 1 to 2 per page-equivalent in running prose flags over-reliance.
7. **Combined pause-punctuation signal** (`G.pause-mark-count`). After judging em-dashes, colons, and semicolons individually, count the three together per page-equivalent as a secondary check. A page within each individual ceiling can still be over-punctuated (3 + 2 + 2 = 7 mid-sentence pause marks). Around 5 or fewer per page in running prose is a reasonable target. Prefer a period when it improves the passage; do not change punctuation solely to lower the count.
8. **Sentence length variance** (`G.sentence-length`). Inspect runs of three sentences within 5 words of each other in length for monotonous rhythm. Preserve natural phrasing and useful parallelism; a similar word count alone requires no edit.
9. **Opening / closing patterns** (`G.no-formulaic-openings`, `G.no-formulaic-closings`). Verify that no section starts with a formulaic opener, with an announcement of what follows ("Two constraints shape the design."), or with a paraphrase of the question being answered. Verify that no section ends with "Overall, ..." or "In summary, ...". When `In summary,` / `In conclusion,` / `Overall,` does appear, confirm that it introduces a real synthesis (an enumerated list distilling earlier prose, or a synthesis across multiple data points) rather than restating a single preceding paragraph.
10. **Participial openings** (`G.participial-openings`). Inspect repeated present-participle openings for monotony or an unclear actor. Rewrite the problematic opening, not every occurrence above a count.
11. **Hedging density** (`G.hedge-from-evidence`). If a paragraph contains more than two hedging phrases (e.g., may, might, could, perhaps, to some extent), evaluate whether each is evidence-based. Remove the hedges that are not.
12. **Passive voice** (`G.active-voice`). If more than one-third of sentences in a paragraph are passive, inspect whether the actors or actions are obscured. Prefer active voice when it improves the passage; preserve a passive that suits its focus.
13. **Paragraph length** (`G.paragraph-length`). Inspect every one- or two-sentence paragraph in sustained running prose for a clear structural purpose. More than one or two on a page, or any run of three or more, prompts a check for fragmentation. Combine paragraphs that develop the same point, or develop an incomplete point using existing information. Keep a short paragraph when merging it would obscure a section transition, a list or figure introduction, or a self-contained qualification. Rewrite a bare fragment as connected prose. Do not add filler, split sentences artificially, or combine unrelated topics to reach a sentence count. List items, captions, and complete short replies follow their own format.
14. **American English spelling** (`G.american-english`). Search for common British variants: analyse, behaviour, organisation, modelling, colour, favour, centre, defence, licence. Replace with American equivalents.
15. **Redundancy check** (`G.refer-back`). Scan for definitions, points, or findings stated in full more than once. Replace duplicated content with a reference back to where it is first stated. One-sentence reminders are fine. Restated paragraphs are not.
16. **Terminology consistency** (`G.one-term`). Verify that each key concept is referred to by the same term throughout. Standardize cases where synonyms are used interchangeably for the same concept.
17. **Hyphenation audit** (`G.compound-hyphens`). Scan for hyphenated noun-noun stacks placed before another noun (e.g., "code-generation benchmarks", "code-translation performance", "data-collection process"). Drop the hyphen unless one element is a participle that would otherwise re-attach as a verb (e.g., "instruction-following ability" stays).
18. **Parenthetical-list signaling** (`G.parenthetical-lists`, `G.one-example-signal`). Scan for `(X, Y, Z)` patterns with 2+ comma-separated items. Verify that each is signaled as examples (`e.g.,`) or as a definition/full enumeration (`i.e.,`). If neither applies and the list is not obviously exhaustive, add `e.g.,`. Also flag any parenthetical that mixes `e.g.,` with `etc.`, or that uses `etc.` while the rest of the document signals examples with `e.g.,`. Pick one signal per document and apply it consistently. Skip abbreviation expansions such as "large language models (LLMs)".
19. **Concision** (`G.sentence-padding`, `G.paragraph-padding`). Scan for sentence-level padding (multi-word filler such as "in order to", "due to the fact that", "has the ability to"; empty lead-ins such as "it is important to note that"; redundant doublets such as "end result") and replace or delete it. Then scan each paragraph for windup sentences (a section-opening paragraph that states the claim of the section or orients the reader across its subsections is not one) and for sentences that restate a predecessor without adding a claim, qualification, evidence, or a needed connection, and cut the redundant sentences. Preserve transitions that connect the paragraph to the preceding argument. Count generic-truth sentences, aphorisms, and evaluative sentences. More than one in a paragraph, or more than two or three per page-equivalent, is filler. Apply the subtractive test. If deleting a word, clause, or sentence costs no meaning, emphasis, precision, or continuity, delete it.
20. **List-cramming** (`G.no-list-cramming`). Scan for any single sentence that packs three or more independent clauses as a run-in series (typically a colon or dash, then semicolon-joined clauses, each often with its own citation). Break it into connected sentences that preserve the relations among the claims, using ordinals only for actual stages or numbered items, or reduce the items to short parallel fragments so a colon introduces a true list. Two linked clauses, or a colon introducing one payoff clause, do not count.
21. **Plain, literal language** (`G.no-coinages`, `G.plain-words`, `G.no-figurative-language`). Scan for (a) invented hyphenated compounds and verbs-used-as-nouns (including coinages naming a specific artifact or document, hyphenated or not, such as "track-changes file" / "data sources table", the bare count nouns "a write", "a read", "a delete", and abstract labels built from a domain word plus a problem-noun such as paradox, trap, creep, divide, vacuum, or inversion), (b) colorful or insider synonyms that have a plainer equivalent, including "gate" or "checkpoint" used instead of naming what must pass, and (c) idioms, metaphorical framing, and personification, that is, a desire, feeling, belief, duty, or merit predicated of code, a model, data, a document, a sentence, a punctuation mark, a tool, a method, or a rule. Treat each as a candidate and apply `G.context-first`. Rewrite when the phrase obscures a mechanism, exaggerates a claim, or contributes no meaning. Keep clear conventional framing, an apt metaphor, an established term, and literal code/API/config identifiers. A sample-word match alone is not a finding.
22. **Literal em-dash glyphs** (`G.em-dash-glyphs`). Search for the literal `—` (U+2014). Replace with `---` in LaTeX; with `:`, `-`, or a table in Markdown/plain text. Exclude en dashes in ranges, minus signs, and `—` inside quotes or code.
23. **Example/restatement connectives** (`G.connectives`). For each em-dash or colon flagged under `G.em-dashes` and `G.colons`, check what it introduces before collapsing it to a period or comma. If it introduces an example, prefer such as, e.g., for example, or including. If it introduces a restatement or definition, prefer i.e., that is, in other words, or namely. Leave alone a mark that sets up a payoff clause or introduces a true list, and keep the example signal consistent per `G.one-example-signal`.
24. **Reference** (`G.anchor-pronouns`, `G.summarizing-nouns`, `G.definite-article`, `G.stand-ins`). For each sentence-initial *This*, *These*, *That*, or *It* and each *such/this/these* + noun, apply the "[noun] just mentioned" test. Attach or repeat the noun where the reference is unclear, and replace a summarizing noun that names nothing that the text listed. For each *the* + noun at first mention, ask "which one?" and drop the definite when the preceding text cannot answer. Replace a stand-in word (*ones*, *those*, *the former / the latter*, *respectively*, *do so*) when its referent is unclear or the lookup obstructs reading. Keep immediate, understandable references. Skip dummy *it*.
25. **Clause boundaries** (`G.keep-that`, `G.whose-on-things`, `G.possessive-on-things`). In formal prose, restore *that* between two adjacent noun phrases and after a reporting verb when the following noun phrase is a clause subject. Leave fixed heads, quoted text, and deliberately informal text alone. For each *whose* with a thing as its head, restate the condition with *with*, a participle or verb on the head, or a clause with the property as its subject, and keep the restatement when it loses nothing. As a secondary signal, more than one *whose* per page-equivalent of running prose flags the habit. For each 's on a thing or an abstraction, prefer the of-phrase, a preposition, or a noun adjunct that names the relation. Keep 's for a person, group, organization, or named tool, for a document standing for its authors, in fixed phrases, and where the possessor is the topic and the of-phrase would move it to the end. Inspect a head noun that never takes the of-form and any phrase with two possessives.
26. **Announced counts** (`G.no-announced-counts`). Scan for sentences that state how many items follow. Keep the count where a later sentence refers back to it, or where the items are an enumerated specification; a checklist; the conditions, phases, or research questions of a study; or a contribution list. Delete it elsewhere and state the first item.
27. **Prose listicles** (`G.no-prose-listicle`). Scan for consecutive paragraphs opened by ordinals that number an unmarked list. Set independent items as a real list, or drop the ordinals and connect the claim of each paragraph to the developing argument. Keep ordinals within a paragraph when they identify actual stages or numbered items.
28. **Consequence connectives** (`G.consequence-connectives`). Delete sentence-initial "So" when it is an empty answer opener. Keep a needed consequence marker, and consider moving sentence-initial "Therefore," or "Thus," into mid-position when that reads more naturally. Count so, therefore, thus, hence, consequently, and as a result together per page-equivalent, and read any run of consecutive sentences that use one of them. Above two or three per page, or where one word carries nearly all of them at that density, inspect whether the connectives add needed links. Delete a connective only when the relation remains clear without extra effort. Recast with because or since where the cause reads better after the effect, and match the remaining connectives to the relation. Substitute one word for another last. Leave parallelism across items of the same kind alone. Skip the intensifier, "so that", the correlative "so ... that", and quoted material.
29. **Empty nouns and verbs** (`G.be-concrete`). Scan for thing, stuff, aspect, element, factor, area, item, component, piece, part, issue, concept, and entity as a head noun, and for a number of, various, certain, and several. First determine whether the noun is an empty placeholder or a useful abstraction, frame, or summary. Use a specific noun when the reader needs that referent. If an essential referent cannot be determined, record the finding under author judgment rather than guessing. Do not replace a purpose statement with a list of topics. Apply the same test to an empty verb phrase. In revision descriptions, scan for "moved" and "shifted" and name the actual change or current behavior, using only what the source establishes. Keep version history when it helps comparison, upgrading, reproduction, or understanding a change. Keep actual relocation with a named source and destination, physical movement, and established technical senses. Keep the generic noun where it contrasts with another category, carries an established technical sense, or names a category that the text defined.
30. **Context and purpose** (`G.context-first`). Read the surrounding paragraph, state the concrete benefit of a proposed stylistic edit, and check that it preserves purpose, logical relations, scope, emphasis, and voice. Keep the original when the difference is only a preference for shorter, more literal, or more explicit wording. Apply explicit conventions as conventions rather than labeling every departure as AI slop.
31. **Continuity and progression** (`G.connected-prose`). Reread adjacent sentences and paragraphs together, especially after splitting sentences or removing transitions. Check that readers can follow how each point develops the question or purpose. Where "then", "next", or ordinals merely count statements, explain the supported relation between them. Preserve useful conjunctions, subordinate clauses, references, and transitions. Keep genuine procedural order, and do not invent a causal link or stronger conclusion to make the prose flow.
