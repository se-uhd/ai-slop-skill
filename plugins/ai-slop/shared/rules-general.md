# Writing rules: general layer

This file is the base layer of the writing rules. It applies to any prose, scientific
or not, in any format. Two further layers build on it: `rules-scientific.md`
adds conventions for empirical research articles, and `rules-latex.md` adds
mechanics for LaTeX source. A skill loads the layers its scope calls for
(general only; general + scientific; or all three).

These rules target AI slop and the habits that make machine-written prose
recognizable, plus universal points of clean writing. The general AI-trope list
(banned words, formulaic openings, formatting tics, anaphora and tricolon abuse)
is fetched separately from the upstream catalog. This layer carries the
restricted-words table with alternatives and a self-check. The rationale behind
the rules that a reader might push back on lives in `rules-rationale.md`, which
the skills do not load. Mechanical rules carry their own short justification or
none.

## Language

- **Use American English consistently** (`G.american-english`). That means analyze (not analyse), behavior (not behaviour), organization (not organisation), modeling (not modelling), color (not colour). Check for British spellings introduced by collaborators or spell-checkers set to the wrong locale.
- **Treat "data" as singular** (`G.data-singular`). "The data shows" and "the data is," not "the data show" or "the data are." Project convention: both forms are accepted in style guides, but we use singular consistently.
- **Use "such as" not "like" to introduce examples** (`G.such-as`). Write "scenarios such as code completion," not "scenarios like code completion." `Like` is colloquial in this construction. `Such as` is the more formal register. Apply this even when the original prose already uses `like`. Do not carry it over verbatim into a rewrite. `Like` remains correct as a verb ("readers like terse prose") and in deliberate similes.

## Restricted Words (Use Sparingly)

**Restricted words** (`G.restricted-words`). Legitimate words that AI overuses. Each use should be intentional. If a simpler word works, use it.

| Restricted | Preferred alternative(s) | When the restricted form is acceptable |
|---|---|---|
| utilize | use | Only when the distinction from "use" matters |
| leverage | use, apply, employ | Only when the lever metaphor is intentional |
| facilitate | enable, support, help, allow | n/a |
| foster | encourage, support, promote | n/a |
| streamline | simplify, reduce, speed up | Acceptable in process-improvement contexts |
| underscore | emphasize, highlight, stress | n/a |
| harness | use, apply, employ | n/a |
| encompass | include, cover, span | n/a |
| navigate | handle, manage, deal with | Only for literal movement through a UI, website, menu, or file tree (e.g., "navigate the codebook"); never as a metaphor for handling difficulty ("navigate the challenges") |
| landscape | field, area, environment, situation | n/a |
| nuanced | detailed, subtle, qualified | n/a |
| multifaceted | complex, varied, many-sided | n/a |
| intricate | complex, detailed, elaborate | n/a |
| meticulous | careful, thorough, rigorous | n/a |
| transformative | n/a | Only when genuinely describing transformation |

**Standard vocabulary (novel, robust, comprehensive, insights, mitigate, enhance, innovative, paradigm, framework, stakeholder, fundamentally, inherently)** is not restricted. Do not overuse it; if a simpler word works equally well, prefer it.

**Transition words (moreover, furthermore, notably, conversely, crucially, etc.)** are also standard and not restricted. Do not overuse them. If more than two appear in a single paragraph, check whether any can be dropped without losing logical flow.

**Precedence over the trope catalog** (`G.catalog-precedence`). The runtime-fetched trope catalog flags more words than these rules and lists some of the vocabulary above (e.g., robust, paradigm, framework, notably) among its banned words, just as it flags "In summary"/"In conclusion" that the **No formulaic closings** (`G.no-formulaic-closings`) rule permits in a genuine summary position. Where the catalog and a layer rule disagree, the layer rule wins. The catalog supplies candidates to inspect, and these rules state the deliberate exceptions.

## Phrases to Avoid

**Phrases to avoid** (`G.phrases-to-avoid`). Stock formulations that read as AI filler or stilted register. Replace each with the plain alternative. The trope catalog (fetched separately) covers the broader banned-phrase list. This section holds project-specific additions.

- **"worked example(s)"** -> "example(s)". The qualifier "worked" rarely adds information and reads as padded pedagogical register. Keep it only in the narrow technical sense of a fully solved problem walked through step by step (a textbook "worked solution"), where the contrast with an unsolved exercise is the point.
- **"earn its keep" / "earn its place" / "pull its weight"** -> state what the element contributes (e.g., "the opener introduces the list that follows"). The idiom states a plain judgment as figurative filler.
- **"headline numbers" / "headline figures"** -> name the figures plainly (e.g., "the largest claims", "the most-cited figures", or just "the figures"). "Headline" imports a journalistic, attention-grabbing register that editorializes the numbers instead of stating them.

## Terminology Consistency

- **Use one term for one concept** (`G.one-term`). Once you introduce a term for a concept, use that same term throughout the document. Do not alternate between synonyms for variety. What reads as elegant variation in literary writing creates ambiguity in technical writing. If "code review" is the term, do not switch to "code inspection," "review process," and "code assessment" in subsequent paragraphs.
- **Define terms on first use, then reuse exactly** (`G.define-once`). If a concept needs a definition or explanation, provide it once. After that, the established term carries the meaning without re-explanation.
- **Synonyms are acceptable only for genuinely different concepts** (`G.distinct-synonyms`). If two terms refer to distinct things, use both, but make the distinction explicit.

## Reference

- **Anchor sentence-initial pronouns** (`G.anchor-pronouns`). To test it, replace it with "the [noun] just mentioned". If no noun in the preceding sentence fits, the reference is unanchored. A clause-sized antecedent takes a head noun, as in "The parser accepts unterminated strings. This causes silent data loss." -> "This leniency causes silent data loss." A distant or contested antecedent takes the noun itself, as in "The compiler emits a warning when the cache is stale. It is disabled by default." -> "The warning is disabled by default." Repeating the noun is the correct fix in technical prose (see **Use one term for one concept** (`G.one-term`)). Exempt: dummy *it* ("it is unclear whether", "it follows that") and a demonstrative that already carries its noun ("this study").
- **A summarizing noun must name something the text has listed** (`G.summarizing-nouns`). *such tools*, *this approach*, *these challenges* after a passage that names no tools, approach, or challenges refer to a category the text never introduced. Use the noun for what was listed, or introduce the category, as in "LLMs can generate unit tests, triage bug reports, and summarize pull requests. Such tools are already in industrial use." -> "Tools for these tasks are already in industrial use." Same test as above.
- **Definite article only for known referents** (`G.definite-article`). A first mention takes *a/an*, the zero article for mass and plural nouns, or an explicit introduction. *The* is for something already mentioned, unique in context, or entailed by something mentioned. To test it, ask "which one?". If the text so far cannot answer, drop the definite, as in "He built a test generator and the rerun infrastructure to detect flaky tests." -> "He built a test generator and infrastructure to rerun the generated tests and detect which are flaky." A purpose or description after the noun ("the tooling to support X") does not by itself identify it. Exempt: postmodifiers that single out one referent ("the version we used"), nouns unique by nature or convention ("the compiler", "the Linux kernel"), and generic definites ("the developer sees a warning").
- **No stand-in words** (`G.stand-ins`). A stand-in word for a noun or clause the sentence could state directly (*ones*, *those*, *the former / the latter*, *the same*, *respectively*, *do so*) costs the reader a lookup. To test it, put the noun back. If it now repeats within the clause, restructure the clause rather than keep the stand-in. "detect flaky tests among the generated ones" -> "detect which of the generated tests are flaky", and "precision and recall were 0.81 and 0.64, respectively" -> "precision was 0.81 and recall 0.64". Keep a stand-in only when the antecedent is in the same clause and the direct form would be longer or still ambiguous.

## Voice

- **Prefer active voice** (`G.active-voice`). Use passive only when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or when passive genuinely reads better. If a sentence works in active voice, use active voice.
- **Use consistent tense within and across adjacent paragraphs** (`G.consistent-tense`). Unmotivated tense shifts are distracting. Switch tense only when the temporal frame genuinely changes.
- **Participial openings** (`G.participial-openings`). Open at most one sentence per page with a present-participle clause ("-ing, ..."). The construction is a high-frequency AI opener.

## Clause Boundaries

- **Keep *that* in formal prose** (`G.keep-that`). Omitting the relativizer of a restrictive object relative ("the metrics the benchmark reports") or the complementizer after a reporting verb ("the results show the effect persists") is grammatical but conversational. Formal prose keeps it, and AI text drops it. Two shapes to scan for are two noun phrases in a row with nothing between them, and a verb that can take a direct object (show, find, confirm, report, reveal, suggest, indicate, ensure, mean, assume, argue, conclude) followed by a noun phrase that turns out to be a clause subject, which the reader first parses as the object. Restore *that*, or *who* for people, as in "the metrics that the benchmark reports" and "the results show that the effect persists." Exempt: fixed heads ("the way we", "the reason we", "the first time we"), quoted text, and deliberately informal text (commit messages, code comments). Non-restrictive *which* is never omitted and needs no check.

## Punctuation

- **Em-dashes** (`G.em-dashes`). Judge each dash on its own. Is an em-dash genuinely the right mark, or is it standing in for a period, comma, or parentheses that would read better? Reserve it for a genuine interruption or an appositive that needs setting off. If splitting into two sentences or using a comma works, do that, and never swap an em-dash for parentheses, since both signal a sentence doing too much. Count is only a secondary signal. More than 2 to 3 per page-equivalent (~350 words) of running prose flags over-reliance and is worth a second pass, but one dash used wrongly is a worse problem than three used well. Two structural uses are legitimate and are not over-reliance: (a) appositives whose interior already contains commas, where outer commas around the appositive would be ambiguous (`Three reviewers — Alice, Bob, and Carol — flagged the issue`); and (b) a parenthetical that itself contains parentheses, where em dashes stand in for an outer pair of parens to avoid nesting (`the corpus — built from public repositories (GitHub, GitLab) — covers ...`). Em dashes inside quoted source material likewise do not count, since they belong to the quotation, not to the writer.
- **Literal em-dash glyphs in source** (`G.em-dash-glyphs`). A literal `—` (U+2014) in code-edited source is a tell on its own. Editors don't autocorrect `-`/`--` into `—`, so humans type `-`, `--`, or `:` (and `---` in LaTeX). Flag every literal `—` in Markdown, plain text, comments, or `.tex`, and replace it with the format's ASCII form. The common case is a uniform `term — gloss` list separator (API references, glossaries). The list is fine, the glyph is not. Prefer `term: gloss`, a hyphen, or a table. This rule is about the glyph, not the per-mark judgment in **Em-dashes** (`G.em-dashes`) above.
- **Colons** (`G.colons`). Judge each colon. It is appropriate before a list, a definition, or a deliberate setup-and-payoff that justifies the pause, not as a generic mid-sentence pause. If a colon could be replaced by a period with light rewriting, use the period. Two failure modes to watch for. First, colon-followed-by-list patterns appearing in every other paragraph. Second, colons that have crept in as substitutes for em-dashes or parentheses when those were forbidden. Count is a secondary signal. More than 2 per page-equivalent in running prose flags over-reliance. Headings, table captions, and `figure:`-style labels are exempt. The rule applies to running prose.
- **Colon before a list or continuation** (`G.introducer-colon`). When a sentence introduces an enumerated list, a numbered structure (e.g., `(1) ... (2) ... (3) ...`), or a paragraph that grammatically completes or elaborates the introducer, end the introducer with `:`, not `.`. AI text systematically defaults to `.` here. To test it, look at what follows. If the following block is a list or directly continues the introducer, use `:`. If it stands as an independent sentence on its own topic, the period is correct. Introducer colons doing this grammatical job are exempt from the colon count above, which targets mid-sentence colons used as filler pauses, em-dash substitutes, or decorative setup.
- **Capitalization after a colon** (`G.colon-capitalization`). In running prose, capitalize the first word after a colon when what follows is a complete sentence (an independent clause). Keep it lowercase when the colon introduces a fragment, phrase, single word, or list. Examples: "We adopt one rule: Capitalize after a colon when a full sentence follows." vs. "We considered three options: speed, accuracy, and cost." Headings and `figure:` / `table:` labels are exempt. The capitalization is a project convention. AI prose reliably defaults to lowercase here regardless of clause completeness. When a colon introduces an enumerated series rather than a single payoff clause, treat it as a list and keep the first word lowercase even if the items are themselves independent clauses. Prefer splitting a series of three or more clauses into sentences (see **No list-cramming in a single sentence** (`G.no-list-cramming`)), which removes the question.
- **Semicolons** (`G.semicolons`). Judge each semicolon. It is appropriate to join two closely related independent clauses where the connection adds meaning a period would lose, or to separate items in a complex list where commas would create ambiguity. It is not a generic mid-sentence pause or a default substitute for a period when both clauses can stand alone. If it could be replaced by a period with no loss, use the period. Two separate sentences are often more natural. The most common misuse is a semicolon that glues a second independent clause onto the first, often one opening with a pronoun (we, it, this, they, these). Default to a period there. Count is a secondary signal. More than 1 to 2 per page-equivalent in running prose flags over-reliance.
- **Name the relation with a connective** (`G.connectives`). When a flagged em-dash or colon is introducing an example or a restatement, the precise fix is usually the connective that names that relation, not a bare period or comma. Use an example connective (such as, e.g., for example, including) when what follows illustrates, and a restatement connective (i.e., that is, in other words, namely) when what follows renames, defines, or restates the same thing in different words. These connectives are standard in academic prose and under-used by AI text, which defaults to a dash or colon instead, so naming the relation both sharpens the meaning and removes a tell. Two guards. First, apply it only when the mark is genuinely doing this work. A dash or colon that sets up a single payoff clause (see **Colons** (`G.colons`)) or introduces a true list is neither an example nor a restatement, and stays as is. Second, keep the choice consistent with the parenthetical-list rules below: pick one example signal (`e.g.,` or `such as`) and stay with it, and do not pair `e.g.,` with a trailing `etc.`
- **Combined pause-mark count** (`G.pause-mark-count`). Em-dashes, colons, and semicolons all create mid-sentence pauses, and AI text shifts the load between them when any single mark is restricted, so judging each mark alone misses the pattern. The primary test stays per mark. Is each pause the right choice? As a combined secondary signal, a page near all three individual ceilings at once (say 3 em-dashes, 2 colons, 2 semicolons) is over-punctuated even though no single ceiling is exceeded. For each mark, ask whether a split into two sentences reads better.
- **Sentence length** (`G.sentence-length`). Vary deliberately. Mix short sentences (5 to 10 words) with longer compound sentences (25 to 35 words). AI text is detectable by its uniformity (around 15 to 25 words per sentence, low burstiness). Do not homogenize sentence length when editing.
- **Hyphenation of compound modifiers** (`G.compound-hyphens`). AI text over-hyphenates noun-noun stacks placed before another noun ("code-generation benchmarks", "code-translation performance", "prompt-format sensitivity", "data-collection process", "model-evaluation pipeline"). These three-noun stacks are usually unambiguous without the hyphen ("code generation benchmarks"). Drop it. Keep the hyphen only when the unhyphenated form invites a real misread, typically when one element is a participle that could re-attach as a verb ("instruction-following ability" stays hyphenated because "instruction following ability" briefly reads as "instruction following" + "ability" with "following" as a verb). Genuine compound adjectives where one element is an adjective or participle (high-quality, well-known, large-scale, fine-grained) keep the hyphen. This rule adjusts the hyphen of a legitimate compound only; an invented compound such as "review-flattening" is a coinage to rewrite, not a hyphen to drop (see **Plain, literal language**).
- **Oxford comma** (`G.oxford-comma`). Use it, but do not let comma-heavy constructions substitute for clearer sentence structure.

## Structure

- **No formulaic section openings** (`G.no-formulaic-openings`). Never open a section or subsection with "In today's...", "In the realm of...", "In an era of...", "As we explore...", or "[Topic] has become increasingly...". Start with the substance.
- **No formulaic closings** (`G.no-formulaic-closings`). Do not use "Overall, ...," "In summary, ...," or "In conclusion, ..." to close a single paragraph by restating what it already said. These phrases are acceptable when they do genuine work: "In summary" and "In conclusion" in actual summary or conclusion sections; "Overall" when synthesizing across multiple data points or conditions (e.g., "Overall, the pattern held across all three groups"); and "In summary, ..." when introducing an enumerated list that consolidates several preceding paragraphs of prose into a checklist (e.g., "In summary, our recommendation is to report: (1) X; (2) Y; (3) Z."). The diagnostic is whether the phrase consolidates content the reader has not yet seen in list form. If the next construct is a numbered or bulleted list distilling earlier prose, the opener is justified. If it introduces a sentence or two that just restates the paragraph above, it is filler.
- **Paragraph length** (`G.paragraph-length`). A paragraph normally contains more than one sentence. Avoid the AI tic of breaking prose into one-sentence paragraphs for manufactured emphasis. A one-sentence paragraph is acceptable when it serves a clear structural purpose, such as opening a section with a framing or topic sentence, introducing an enumerated list or figure, marking a section transition, or carrying a topic sentence that genuinely stands alone. The diagnostic is whether absorbing the paragraph into its neighbor would lose something the prose needs. Flag any page with more than one or two single-sentence paragraphs, or any run of three or more consecutive single-sentence paragraphs.
- **No rule-of-three defaults** (`G.no-rule-of-three`). Do not reflexively group things in threes. If there are two items, list two. If there are four, list four.
- **Keep enumerations short** (`G.short-enumerations`). Examples introduced by "e.g.,", "such as", "including", or numbered markers like "(1) ... (2) ... (3) ..." should illustrate, not exhaustively iterate. Two or three representative items are almost always enough. If the full set matters, use a table, figure, or dedicated list. Do not pack it into a parenthetical or running sentence. When in doubt, cut the enumeration to the clearest examples and stop.
- **No list-cramming in a single sentence** (`G.no-list-cramming`). Do not flatten three or more independent clauses into one sentence as a run-in series. The giveaway shape is a colon or dash opening a chain of semicolon-joined clauses, each often carrying its own citation or parenthetical, so that one sentence carries what should be several and the density reads as a list crammed into prose to avoid a line break. Break the chain into separate sentences (a plain sequence, or `First, ... Second, ... Third, ...` when the count itself matters). Only when the items reduce to short parallel fragments, keep the colon so it introduces a true list ("three options: speed, accuracy, and cost"). Two tightly linked independent clauses joined by a semicolon are fine, and a colon or dash introducing a single payoff clause is fine. The target is the three-or-more clause pile-up, not the punctuation mark. This rule governs not welding a list of clauses into one grammatical sentence, which is distinct from **Keep enumerations short** (`G.short-enumerations`) (illustrative-list length) and **No rule-of-three defaults** (`G.no-rule-of-three`) (item count).
- **Mark parenthetical lists as examples or exhaustive** (`G.parenthetical-lists`). A bare parenthetical with 2+ comma-separated items reads ambiguously. The reader cannot tell whether the list is representative or complete. Open it with "e.g.," (examples) or "i.e.," (definition or full enumeration), or restructure. The same applies to inline lists in running text introduced by nothing. Use "such as" or "including." Exception: an abbreviation expansion (e.g., "large language models (LLMs)") or a parenthetical that names a single concept.
- **One example signal per document** (`G.one-example-signal`). Don't mix `e.g.,` with `etc.` in the same parenthetical. "(e.g., a, b, etc.)" is redundant. Across a document, prefer `e.g., ...` at the start over `..., etc.` at the end and apply the choice consistently.
- **No excessive bold or formatting** (`G.no-excess-bold`). Do not bold phrases for emphasis in running text. Reserve bold for subsection-level headers or matching the document's existing conventions.
- **Refer back instead of repeating** (`G.refer-back`). State each definition, point, or finding once, in the place where it belongs, and refer back to it from elsewhere rather than restating it. Brief one-sentence reminders are acceptable when needed to follow an argument; full paragraphs of restatement are not. A reader who needs the information again should be pointed to where it lives, not given a second copy.
- **Cut padding at the sentence level** (`G.sentence-padding`). Prefer the shortest phrasing that preserves meaning and precision. Sentence-level padding is distinct from vagueness (`G.be-concrete`), inflated vocabulary (`G.restricted-words`), and duplication (`G.refer-back`). A phrase can be concrete, plain, and unique yet still spend more words than its content needs. Replace multi-word filler with its shorter equivalent ("in order to" -> "to", "due to the fact that" -> "because", "in the event that" -> "if", "has the ability to" -> "can", "the majority of" -> "most", "at this point in time" -> "now"). Delete empty lead-ins ("it is important to note that", "it should be mentioned that", "as can be seen"). Cut redundant doublets where one word already implies the other ("end result", "future plans", "completely eliminate"). The diagnostic is subtractive. If deleting a word or clause costs no meaning, emphasis, or precision, delete it.
- **Cut padding at the paragraph level** (`G.paragraph-padding`). A paragraph should make its point in as many sentences as it needs and no more. Remove windup sentences that announce what the same paragraph then does, and any sentence that restates its predecessor without adding a claim, qualification, or evidence. A paragraph that opens a section is not windup when it states the section's claim or purpose, or when it orients the reader across the subsections with something the headings do not carry (the order, what the parts share, a definition they all need). It is windup when it merely lists the subsection headings or repeats content stated elsewhere (see **Refer back instead of repeating** (`G.refer-back`)). A paragraph that takes five sentences to deliver one sentence of content is padded, even when every sentence is individually clean. Padding is the opposite failure from the **Paragraph length** (`G.paragraph-length`) rule above, which guards against paragraphs cut too short for manufactured emphasis. Generic-truth sentences, true of any document on the subject ("Software testing is a critical part of software development"), and evaluative sentences that grade the preceding claim without adding to it ("This is an important finding") are legitimate in small numbers and filler in quantity. One of either can open a section or mark that a result matters. Flag a paragraph with more than one, or a page-equivalent (~350 words) with more than two or three in total. Where the paragraph already states the claim, fold the grade into it.
- **Reformulate, do not delete** (`G.reformulate`). When prose is flagged, the default fix is to rewrite it into a clear, correct form that keeps the author's intended content. Delete only genuine filler (the padding, windup sentences, and non-evidence-based hedges the rules above target) or when the author explicitly asks for a cut. A flagged claim, example, or qualification carries information that the author chose to include. Resolving the flag by deleting it discards that information silently.

## Tone

- **Match the existing document's voice** (`G.match-voice`). Read the draft before writing. Mirror its register, its level of formality, and how it handles transitions.
- **Be concrete** (`G.be-concrete`). Instead of "provides valuable insights into X," state the actual finding.
- **Hedge only from evidence** (`G.hedge-from-evidence`). "The data suggests X" is appropriate when the data is ambiguous. "One could argue that X" is filler unless you then explain who argues it and why.
- **Take positions** (`G.take-positions`). When the evidence points one direction, say so. Do not present artificial balance.
- **No author-voice metacommentary in published prose** (`G.no-metacommentary`). Asides like "reader beware," "the reader is warned," "we shall see," "spoiler alert," or "(more on this below)" break the formal register and address the reader directly about the act of reading. Remove them from rendered body text, or move them into editorial notes that do not render. Treat them as a clear finding when they appear in body text, not as a stylistic judgment call. Forward references to later sections are fine as plain pointers, not as winks.

## Plain, literal language

- **Use plain, literal language** (`G.plain-language`). Three habits make prose read as machine-written without adding meaning. The fix for each is the plain version. Each has an exception for genuinely precise usage.
  - **No invented compounds or verbs-as-nouns** (`G.no-coinages`). Do not join domain words into hyphenated modifiers used as if they were established terms ("review-flattening", "new-entity creation", "data-integrity gating"), and do not turn a verb into a noun ("the delegate's own clear", "a full delete", "the ask"), including the systems-jargon count nouns "a write", "a read", "a create", and "a delete" (as in "each write", "the failed write", "repeat the create", or "reads and writes"). Rewrite to the verb or phrase the coinage compressed: "review-flattening" -> "flattening the review"; "the delegate's own clear" -> "the delegate's own call to clear it"; "a write" -> "a write request" (or "a write operation"). The same pattern covers noun phrases coined ad hoc to name a specific artifact, document, or table, with or without a hyphen ("data sources table" and "track changes file" are the same coinages unhyphenated). Describe what the thing is instead: "track-changes file" -> "the accompanying PDF" (or "the latexdiff PDF"); "data-sources table" -> "a table listing all data sources". Dropping the hyphen is not enough, and the **Hyphenation of compound modifiers** (`G.compound-hyphens`) participle exception does not cover an invented gerund compound. Keep established terms and literal code, API, or config identifiers as written ("foreign-key constraint", a `user_secrets` key, a `git fetch`).
  - **Plain word over a colorful or insider synonym** (`G.plain-words`). Replace a vivid or niche word with the plain one when it adds no precision: create not "mint", show or report not "surface", combine not "weave", guide not "shepherd", merge not "land", coordinate not "orchestrate", enable not "unlock". Keep the word only for a distinction the plain one loses: "exercise" for driving inputs through a specific code path, not for "run"; "seam" for a Feathers seam, not for any interface. These words are a sample of an open pattern, not a closed list.
  - **No figurative language** (`G.no-figurative-language`). Replace idioms and clichés with the literal claim ("low-hanging fruit" -> "the easiest improvements"; likewise "move the needle", "boils down to", "a double-edged sword", "earn its keep"), drop metaphorical framing ("the data tells a story"; "X is the backbone of Y" -> "Y depends on X"), and do not personify code, models, tests, or data ("the model wants to" -> "the model is trained to"; "the test is unhappy" -> "the test fails"). Two exceptions: established technical terms whose etymology is metaphorical are literal domain vocabulary (tree, branch, thread, stream, pipeline, fork, deadlock, mock, stub); and one analogy that genuinely helps a reader understand an unfamiliar concept is fine when introduced once, marked as an analogy, and not continued through the text.

## Self-Check Before Presenting Text

Before presenting any written or edited text, scan it against these checks:

1. **Word-level scan** (`G.restricted-words`, `G.catalog-precedence`). Search for banned words (per tropes.fyi) and replace any that appear, except vocabulary these rules explicitly leave unrestricted (see **Precedence over the trope catalog** (`G.catalog-precedence`)). Count restricted words (per the table above). If more than 2 to 3 appear in a single paragraph, rewrite to reduce.
2. **Em-dashes** (`G.em-dashes`). Check each em-dash. Is it the right mark, or would a period, comma, or parentheses read better? Replace each em-dash that a period, comma, or parentheses would serve better. As a secondary signal, more than 2 to 3 per page-equivalent (~350 words) of running prose flags over-reliance. Exclude from any count: em dashes inside quoted source material, and glyphs that are not em dashes at all, such as en dashes in numeric or page ranges (`pp. 12–18`), minus signs in math, and hyphens or `--` sequences inside code listings. Also exclude the two structural exceptions noted in the **Em-dashes** (`G.em-dashes`) rule (comma-bearing appositives and nested-parens avoidance).
3. **Colons** (`G.colons`). Examine each colon. Any that could be replaced by a period with light rewriting should be. Watch especially for colons substituting for em-dashes or parentheses, and for colon-followed-by-list patterns repeating across paragraphs. As a secondary signal, more than 2 per page-equivalent in running prose flags over-reliance.
4. **Introducer punctuation** (`G.introducer-colon`). For every sentence immediately followed by an enumerated list, a numbered structure (e.g., `(1) ... (2) ... (3) ...`), a bulleted block, or a paragraph that grammatically continues or elaborates it, verify the introducer ends with `:`, not `.`. AI text systematically defaults to `.` here. The `:` is correct and does not count toward the colon signal.
5. **Capitalization after a colon** (`G.colon-capitalization`). For every colon in running prose, check whether the text that follows is a complete sentence. If it is, the first word must be capitalized. If it is a fragment, phrase, single word, or list, keep the first word lowercase. Fix mismatches. For a colon introducing an enumerated series of independent clauses, keep the first word lowercase (it is a list) and prefer splitting three or more such clauses into sentences (see `G.no-list-cramming`).
6. **Semicolons** (`G.semicolons`). Examine each semicolon. Replace it with a period and two sentences when both clauses can stand alone. Reserve semicolons for genuine list disambiguation and for tightly coupled independent clauses where the link adds meaning a period would lose. As a secondary signal, more than 1 to 2 per page-equivalent in running prose flags over-reliance.
7. **Combined pause-punctuation signal** (`G.pause-mark-count`). After judging em-dashes, colons, and semicolons individually, count the three together per page-equivalent as a secondary check. A page within each individual ceiling can still be over-punctuated (3 + 2 + 2 = 7 mid-sentence pause marks). Around 5 or fewer per page in running prose is a reasonable target. Prefer a period whenever the sentence allows.
8. **Sentence length variance** (`G.sentence-length`). If three consecutive sentences are within 5 words of each other in length, revise at least one.
9. **Opening / closing patterns** (`G.no-formulaic-openings`, `G.no-formulaic-closings`). Verify no section starts with a formulaic opener or ends with "Overall, ..." or "In summary, ...". When `In summary,` / `In conclusion,` / `Overall,` does appear, confirm it introduces a real synthesis (an enumerated list distilling earlier prose, or a synthesis across multiple data points) rather than restating a single preceding paragraph.
10. **Participial openings** (`G.participial-openings`). If more than one sentence per page starts with a present participle clause ("-ing, ..."), rewrite.
11. **Hedging density** (`G.hedge-from-evidence`). If a paragraph contains more than two hedging phrases (may, might, could, perhaps, to some extent), evaluate whether each is evidence-based. Remove the hedges that are not.
12. **Passive voice** (`G.active-voice`). If more than one-third of sentences in a paragraph are passive, rewrite the avoidable sentences in active voice.
13. **Paragraph length** (`G.paragraph-length`). Count single-sentence paragraphs per page. More than one or two on a page, or any run of three or more in a row, is a flag. Fold each into a neighbor unless it does work that absorbing it would lose, such as opening a section, introducing a list or figure, marking a section transition, or carrying a stand-alone topic sentence.
14. **American English spelling** (`G.american-english`). Search for common British variants: analyse, behaviour, organisation, modelling, colour, favour, centre, defence, licence (verb). Replace with American equivalents.
15. **Redundancy check** (`G.refer-back`). Scan for definitions, points, or findings stated in full more than once. Replace duplicated content with a reference back to where it is first stated. One-sentence reminders are fine; restated paragraphs are not.
16. **Terminology consistency** (`G.one-term`). Verify each key concept is referred to by the same term throughout. Standardize cases where synonyms are used interchangeably for the same concept.
17. **Hyphenation audit** (`G.compound-hyphens`). Scan for hyphenated noun-noun stacks placed before another noun (e.g., "code-generation benchmarks", "code-translation performance", "data-collection process"). Drop the hyphen unless one element is a participle that would otherwise re-attach as a verb (e.g., "instruction-following ability" stays).
18. **Parenthetical-list signaling** (`G.parenthetical-lists`, `G.one-example-signal`). Scan for `(X, Y, Z)` patterns with 2+ comma-separated items. Verify each is signaled as examples (`e.g.,`) or as a definition/full enumeration (`i.e.,`); if neither and the list isn't obviously exhaustive, add `e.g.,`. Also flag any parenthetical that mixes `e.g.,` with `etc.`, or that uses `etc.` while the rest of the document signals examples with `e.g.,`. Pick one signal per document and apply it consistently. Skip abbreviation expansions like "large language models (LLMs)".
19. **Concision** (`G.sentence-padding`, `G.paragraph-padding`). Scan for sentence-level padding (multi-word filler such as "in order to", "due to the fact that", "has the ability to"; empty lead-ins such as "it is important to note that"; redundant doublets such as "end result") and replace or delete it. Then scan each paragraph for windup sentences (a section-opening paragraph that states the section's claim or orients the reader across its subsections is not one) and for sentences that restate a predecessor without adding a claim, qualification, or evidence, and cut them. Count generic-truth and evaluative sentences. More than one in a paragraph, or more than two or three per page-equivalent, is filler. Apply the subtractive test. If deleting a word, clause, or sentence costs no meaning, emphasis, or precision, delete it.
20. **List-cramming** (`G.no-list-cramming`). Scan for any single sentence that packs three or more independent clauses as a run-in series (typically a colon or dash, then semicolon-joined clauses, each often with its own citation). Break it into separate sentences, or reduce the items to short parallel fragments so a colon introduces a true list. Two linked clauses, or a colon introducing one payoff clause, do not count.
21. **Plain, literal language** (`G.no-coinages`, `G.plain-words`, `G.no-figurative-language`). Scan for (a) invented hyphenated compounds and verbs-used-as-nouns (including coinages naming a specific artifact or document, hyphenated or not, such as "track-changes file" / "data sources table", and the bare count nouns "a write", "a read", "a delete"), (b) colorful or insider synonyms that have a plainer equivalent, and (c) idioms, metaphorical framing, and personification of code, models, or data. Rewrite each in plain, literal form unless it is an established term, a literal code/API/config identifier, a word carrying a precise distinction, or one marked analogy. Match the named seed words even when nothing reads wrong; the reviewer may share the writer's blind spot.
22. **Literal em-dash glyphs** (`G.em-dash-glyphs`). Search for the literal `—` (U+2014). Replace with `---` in LaTeX; with `:`, `-`, or a table in Markdown/plain text. Exclude en dashes in ranges, minus signs, and `—` inside quotes or code.
23. **Example/restatement connectives** (`G.connectives`). For each em-dash or colon flagged under `G.em-dashes` and `G.colons`, check what it introduces before collapsing it to a period or comma. If it introduces an example, prefer such as, e.g., for example, or including. If it introduces a restatement or definition, prefer i.e., that is, in other words, or namely. Name the relation when the mark is doing that work. Leave payoff-clause and true-list marks alone, and keep the example signal consistent per `G.parenthetical-lists`.
24. **Reference** (`G.anchor-pronouns`, `G.summarizing-nouns`, `G.definite-article`, `G.stand-ins`). For each sentence-initial *This*, *These*, *That*, or *It* and each *such/this/these* + noun, apply the "[noun] just mentioned" test. Attach or repeat the noun, and replace a summarizing noun that names nothing the text listed. For each *the* + noun at first mention, ask "which one?" and drop the definite when the preceding text cannot answer. Replace each stand-in word (*ones*, *those*, *the former / the latter*, *respectively*, *do so*) with its noun, and restructure the clause if the noun then repeats. Skip dummy *it*.
25. **Clause boundaries** (`G.keep-that`). In formal prose, restore *that* between two adjacent noun phrases and after a reporting verb whose following noun phrase is a clause subject. Leave fixed heads, quoted text, and deliberately informal text alone.
